# The History of Coffee

Coffee's story begins in the highlands of Ethiopia, where the coffee plant grows wild and where
legend credits a goatherd named Kaldi with noticing his goats' energy after eating the cherries.
By the 15th century, coffee cultivation and trade had taken hold in Yemen, and Sufi monasteries
used the brew to stay awake through night prayers. From the port of Mocha it spread across the
Islamic world.

Coffeehouses, or qahveh khaneh, became centers of conversation, music, and commerce in cities
like Cairo, Istanbul, and later London and Paris. In 17th-century England these establishments
were nicknamed "penny universities" because a cup bought entry to lively intellectual debate.
The drink was sometimes viewed with suspicion by authorities who feared the political talk it
encouraged, leading to occasional bans that rarely lasted.

European colonial powers transplanted coffee to the Caribbean, South America, and Southeast Asia,
turning it into one of the most traded commodities in the world. Today Brazil and Vietnam lead
global production, and the industry supports millions of smallholder farmers while grappling with
price volatility and climate pressures on growing regions.
